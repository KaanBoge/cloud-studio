\title{Cloud survival in supersonic winds: numerical sensitivity of three-dimensional hydrodynamic calculations}
\author{Kaan Boge}
\affiliation{Independent Researcher}
\begin{abstract}
Cold gas carried through a hot galactic wind can be compressed, accelerated and dispersed, changing the supply and observable distribution of gas around galaxies. Predicting its survival requires understanding how numerical methods represent the interaction between the two phases. We compare three-dimensional cloud-wind calculations using twelve hydrodynamic methods for a reference cloud-to-wind density contrast of 100, each at three spatial resolutions, with additional grid calculations at contrasts of 10 and 1000. The wind is supersonic, with Mach number 2. At the finest resolution, eleven of the twelve methods retain between 67 and 79 per cent of their initial dense-gas mass after three cloud-crushing times. The methods separate afterwards: after five cloud-crushing times the eight grid methods retain between 10 and 28 per cent, the moving-mesh method 54 per cent, smoothed-particle hydrodynamics 76 per cent, and the meshless finite-volume and finite-mass methods 37 and 98 per cent. Repeating one grid calculation in the periodic domains used for the moving-mesh and particle-based calculations leaves its dense-gas history unchanged for two cloud-crushing times and raises its final dense-gas fraction by 17 to 23 percentage points at the finest resolution. In a common periodic domain this grid calculation ends within 4 percentage points of the meshless finite-volume method, while the moving-mesh, smoothed-particle hydrodynamics and meshless finite-mass methods retain 27 to 64 percentage points more dense gas. The two meshless methods cover similar projected areas with intermediate-temperature gas despite their large difference in dense mass. These results make the late evolution of the cloud-wind interface a central target for controlled comparison.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Galaxies exchange mass, metals and energy with their surroundings through accretion, outflows and recycling. The circumgalactic medium is a substantial gas reservoir in this cycle, with cool and highly ionized material probed along different sightlines \citep{Tumlinson2017}. Absorption-line measurements in the COS-Halos survey establish the widespread presence of cool, photoionized gas around galaxies and show that its inferred density need not follow a simple two-phase pressure-equilibrium model \citep{Werk2014}. Explaining the persistence and spatial distribution of such material requires understanding how dense gas interacts with hotter surroundings. An isolated cloud in a wind provides a tractable experiment for studying that interaction, although it does not reproduce the full structure of a galactic halo.

Classical shock-cloud calculations linked compression, deformation and disruption to a characteristic cloud-crushing time \citep{Klein1994}. Wind calculations extend this problem to sustained momentum transfer: the cloud must gain speed while shocks and interface disturbances redistribute its mass. Radiative simulations demonstrate that both acceleration and mass loss depend on the wind conditions \citep{Scannapieco2015}, while calculations resolving a broad range of thermal phases show that cloud disruption transfers mass and momentum throughout the resulting multiphase flow \citep{Schneider2017}. These studies motivate following the morphology and velocity of selected gas alongside its remaining dense mass. Here $t_{\rm cc}=\sqrt{\chi}R/v_{\rm w}$, where $R$ is the initial cloud radius, $v_{\rm w}$ is the initial wind speed, and $\chi=\rho_{\rm c,0}/\rho_{\rm w,0}$ is the initial density contrast. Times are expressed in this fixed initial unit.

Cooling can alter the direction of mass exchange between phases. Simulations of fountain clouds show that interaction with cooler, enriched material can promote condensation from a hot corona \citep{Armillotta2016}. In cloud-wind calculations, sufficiently rapid cooling of intermediate-temperature gas can support cold-gas growth and entrainment \citep{GronkeOh2018}; the growth and destruction regimes also depend on the surrounding flow and magnetic fields \citep{Sparre2020}. Thermal conduction supplies another competing process, capable of evaporating cloud gas while compressing some clouds into longer-lived structures \citep{Bruggen2016}. A comparison of hydrodynamic methods must therefore distinguish uncertainty in numerical transport from the effects of these additional physical processes. Our principal sample is non-radiative, and a supplementary radiative pair is described in Appendix~\ref{app:cooling}.

Observables place complementary demands on these calculations. Gas mass in a temperature interval does not uniquely determine the area, column density or velocity distribution seen in projection. Conversely, similar projected areas can hide differences in the mass and thermodynamic structure along a sightline. Inferring an ionic absorption signal requires composition, ionization and radiation assumptions in addition to the hydrodynamic fields \citep{Werk2014}. We therefore use column-density morphology and phase-based diagnostics to characterize the simulated gas, without treating a temperature selection as a synthetic ion measurement. The simulations are three-dimensional, allowing disturbances and fragments to evolve in both transverse directions.

The numerical representation of a density interface is a longstanding concern. \citet{Agertz2007} demonstrated substantial differences between grid methods and the standard smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) formulations in their comparison. Modern algorithms provide several distinct treatments of fluid motion and inter-element transport. Arepo solves finite-volume equations on a moving Voronoi mesh \citep{Springel2010}; GIZMO's meshless finite-mass (MFM) and finite-volume (MFV) methods use a meshless volume partition and Riemann-solver fluxes \citep{Hopkins2015}. MFM and MFV differ in whether mass is exchanged between elements. The presence of moving elements alone does not make these schemes equivalent to one another or to SPH, and their names do not specify a common interface resolution.

\citet{Braspenning2023} compared seven non-radiative hydrodynamic methods using dense-gas mass, intermediate-temperature mass and covering fraction. Their results demonstrate that convergence and agreement depend on the diagnostic, motivating a comparison that goes beyond a single survival curve. We adopt this diagnostic emphasis while reporting the differences between our configurations and theirs explicitly. In particular, the relation between particle construction and spatial sampling must be established before a numerical discrepancy can be interpreted as a disagreement between algorithms.

The present study assembles twelve methods across eleven code implementations, including Arepo, eight grid codes, GIZMO MFM and MFV, and GADGET-4 \citep{Springel2021}. The reference sample has $\chi=100$ and three common initial samplings for every method, and additional grid calculations reach $\chi=10$ and 1000. The contribution of this study is the joint record of morphology, mass evolution and selected kinematic and thermal diagnostics across this set of configurations, together with a control that measures the effect of the domain boundaries by running one grid method in the periodic domains of the other methods. The MFV resolution sequence and the late separation of the methods identify concrete targets for controlled follow-up.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the setup and measurements. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents morphology first, followed by mass evolution, the boundary control, the dependence on density contrast and the kinematic and thermal diagnostics. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} examines the MFV and Arepo results in relation to particle spacing, numerical support, transport and the domain boundaries. The appendices give the numerical validation, a supplementary radiative pair and additional AthenaPK calculations.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Cloud and wind}
We place a spherical cloud in a uniform supersonic wind. The gas has adiabatic index $\gamma=5/3$, and the intended initial cloud and wind are in thermal pressure equilibrium. The reference density contrast is $\chi=100$; the additional grid sequences use $\chi=10$ and 1000. With $r$ measured from the cloud centre, the initial density is
\begin{equation}
\rho(r)=\rho_{\rm w,0}\left[1+\frac{\chi-1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\frac{r-R}{0.1R}\right)\right].
\label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
The initial velocity is zero within $1.3R$ and equals the wind velocity outside this radius \citep[see also the initial velocity setup in][]{Gronnow2018}. This prescription is used consistently in the primary figures. The wind Mach number is $\mathcal M=v_{\rm w}/c_{\rm s,w}=2$. The simulations evolve to $5t_{\rm cc}$, with scalar histories normally sampled every $0.05t_{\rm cc}$.

The domain extends from $-3R$ to $17R$ along the wind and from $-5R$ to $5R$ in both transverse directions. The grid calculations impose the wind at the upstream face and outflow at the other faces. The Arepo calculations are periodic along the wind with outflow at the transverse faces, and the GIZMO and GADGET-4 calculations are periodic on all faces. These boundary choices determine how gas and disturbances leave or re-enter the domain. Their effect is measured with a boundary control: the Athena++ \citep{Stone2020} calculation is repeated in each of the two periodic domains at every sampling, with the solver, its options and the initial state unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}). Pressure reconstruction is a further distinction: the initial peak pressure reconstructed by GADGET-4 exceeds the intended value by factors of about 3.90, 2.10 and 1.33 from the coarsest to the finest sampling of Table~\ref{tab:resolution}. Its panel in Figure~\ref{fig:mass} is marked with an asterisk. The Arepo mesh-generating points are displaced from a regular lattice, which gives the initial density and pressure an rms scatter of about 3 per cent around the analytic profile.

\subsection{Spatial resolution and particle construction}
We denote initial sampling by $n$, the number of intervals across a transverse side of length $10R$. The corresponding uniform grid has $2n\times n\times n$ cells, and its spacing is $\Delta x=10R/n$. Table~\ref{tab:resolution} gives both the spacing and the equivalent cubic count, $N^{1/3}$, where $N=2n^3$. All twelve methods are run at $n=32$, 64 and 128.

The finest GIZMO calculations contain 4,194,304 gas elements. Their initializer uses a uniform lattice with variable particle masses $m_i=\rho(r_i)\Delta x^3$. Thus the density contrast is represented by a mass contrast at common initial spacing. The saved preparation record confirms a cloud-to-wind particle-mass ratio approaching 100 and 8,808 particle centres inside $r\leq R$ for the reference case. The input internal energy is assigned from the intended uniform pressure. The solver subsequently reconstructs density from its own volume or kernel estimate, making the reconstructed initial pressure an important diagnostic.

The meshless spatial distribution evolves with the flow. Consequently, the initial spacing specifies the starting sample, whereas the later effective resolution also depends on the local element distribution and reconstruction. The initial density transition has width $0.1R$, corresponding to 1.28 uniform-grid spacings at $n=128$. This narrow interface remains a demanding part of the resolution study.

\subsection{Hydrodynamic methods and comparison groups}
The grid-based sample comprises Athena++ \citep{Stone2020}, AthenaPK on the Parthenon framework \citep{Grete2022}, Athena 4.2 \citep{Stone2008}, Enzo \citep{Bryan2014}, Enzo-E/Cello \citep{Bordner2018}, RAMSES \citep{Teyssier2002}, FLASH 4.8 \citep{Fryxell2000} and Flash-X \citep{Dubey2022}. The remaining methods are the moving-mesh code Arepo \citep{Springel2010}, GIZMO MFM and MFV \citep{Hopkins2015}, and the SPH implementation in GADGET-4 \citep{Springel2021}. MFM and MFV are two methods in one code, giving twelve methods across eleven code implementations.

The reference mass comparison uses $\chi=100$ at $n=32$, 64 and 128 for all twelve methods. The boundary control adds six Athena++ calculations at the same samplings. The contrast extension contains $n=128$ results for Enzo and Enzo-E at $\chi=10$ and 1000, Athena++ at $\chi=10$ and 1000, and AthenaPK at $\chi=10$. Each curve is associated with its actual numerical configuration. The common analytic profile and nominal sampling provide the starting point for comparison, and the boundary control and the reconstructed initial pressure quantify two of the differences that remain between configurations.

We use the published method papers to identify the algorithms, while the saved inputs define the particular calculations. A code name alone does not specify its Riemann solver, reconstruction or diffusion options. Changes to those options require a new calculation. The earlier supplementary AthenaPK pair uses HLLE fluxes, piecewise-linear reconstruction (PLM) and van Leer integration. Its non-radiative $\chi=10$ extension uses the same modified flux correction, with four final fallback activations. Additional completed AthenaPK calculations use HLLC fluxes and piecewise-parabolic reconstruction (PPM): non-radiative $\chi=10$ and 100, and radiative $\chi=100$. The correction source and fallback history distinguish these configurations, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:hllc}.

\subsection{Measurements}\label{sec:measurements}
Dense gas is selected by $\rho>\rho_{\rm c,0}/3$. We sum its mass and normalize by the measured initial dense mass of the same calculation, giving $f_{\rm d}(t)=M_{\rm d}(t)/M_{\rm d}(0)$. This measures the mass satisfying a density criterion at each time. Compression and thermal evolution can change membership of that selection, so an increase in $f_{\rm d}$ can occur during compression.

Morphology panels show total-gas column density integrated along the line of sight, normalized by $\rho_{\rm c,0}R$. Edge-on views look across the wind and face-on views along it; both cover the full domain at the last saved state on one logarithmic colour scale. Grid columns are computed by summing cell contributions. The GIZMO, GADGET-4 and Arepo images deposit the mass of each element at its centre on the $256\times128\times128$ grid of the finest sampling. The deposition conserves mass; the pixel-scale appearance of these images also reflects the deposition scheme.

For the finest MFM and MFV calculations, 11 states separated by $0.5t_{\rm cc}$ provide additional diagnostics. We measure the mass-weighted streamwise velocity of the dense selection. Intermediate-temperature gas is selected using $\chi^{-3/4}<T/T_{\rm w,0}<\chi^{-1/4}$, following the temperature-band diagnostic of \citet{Braspenning2023}. Its mass is normalized by $M_{\rm d}(0)$. For fixed composition, temperature is proportional to specific internal energy; the corresponding grid expression uses $P/\rho$.

The intermediate-temperature covering fraction is the fraction of the full transverse domain above column threshold $0.01(2\rho_{\rm c,0}R)$. We use a $128^2$ image grid. Applying the same column threshold to dense gas gives an area $A_{\rm d}$ and a projected effective radius $R_{\rm eff}=\sqrt{A_{\rm d}/\pi}$. The mean dense-gas contrast is $\chi_{\rm eff}=M_{\rm d}/(\rho_{\rm w,0}V_{\rm d})$, with $V_{\rm d}=\sum_{\rm dense}m_i/\rho_i$. Together with the measured velocity, these give
\begin{equation}
t_{\rm cc,eff}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\chi_{\rm eff}(t)}R_{\rm eff}(t)}{|v_{\rm w}-\langle v_\parallel\rangle_{\rm d}(t)|}.
\label{eq:effective}
\end{equation}
This diagnostic combines the evolving size, density and relative speed using the imposed initial wind as reference. It characterizes the selected gas at each time; determining a disruption time requires following its subsequent evolution. Initial projection and selection effects can make $R_{\rm eff}(0)$ differ from $R$. GIZMO velocities retain their native time staggering.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Late-time morphology}
Figure~\ref{fig:morph} shows the twelve methods at $n=128$ and $5t_{\rm cc}$, and Figure~\ref{fig:morphnew} shows Athena++, Enzo and Enzo-E at contrasts of 10 and 1000. The maps differ in the concentration of the remaining cloud, its position along the wind, the extent of its wake and the structure transverse to the wind. In the eight grid methods the brightest gas lies in the upstream half of the remnant, the bow shock is visible upstream, and behind the remnant the column falls below that of the undisturbed wind. The face-on views of Athena++, AthenaPK, Athena 4.2, RAMSES, FLASH 4.8 and Flash-X are unchanged by a quarter turn about the wind axis and by reflection about the coordinate axes and the diagonals: Athena++ and AthenaPK show a ring crossed by diagonal ridges, Athena 4.2 an eight-armed star, FLASH 4.8 and Flash-X square arrangements of fragments, and RAMSES a smooth ring. Enzo and Enzo-E show irregular fragments. Athena++ and AthenaPK give the same map.

Arepo, MFM and MFV show asymmetric, filamentary remnants, and GADGET-4 retains a compact, nearly round core with a short tail. These four remnants lie further upstream than the grid remnants: where the column exceeds $0.3\rho_{\rm c,0}R$, their upstream edges lie between $3.6R$ and $4.5R$ downstream of the initial cloud centre, and those of the grid remnants between $4.8R$ and $6.6R$. Across the wind the Arepo, MFV and GADGET-4 remnants are narrower than every grid remnant, and the MFM remnant is comparable to the narrowest of them. The GADGET-4 core accompanies the largest dense fraction after MFM (Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}). Fine granularity in the particle and moving-mesh images reflects the deposition of each element at its centre, whereas the larger features reflect the mass distribution across many pixels. In these four maps the ambient column also varies across the domain by up to about 20 per cent, whereas upstream of the bow shock the grid maps keep the wind value. Morphology therefore adds information beyond the amount of gas above a density threshold.

At $\chi=10$ the three clouds remain comparatively coherent at the final time, while the $\chi=1000$ maps have irregular and extended distributions: gas with three times the ambient column reaches the transverse faces of the domain in Athena++ and Enzo-E and its downstream face in Athena++. The Athena++ map at $\chi=1000$ again has four-fold symmetry, with arms along the coordinate axes and the diagonals around a bright central spot, whereas the Enzo and Enzo-E maps are irregular. The background brightness follows the wind density $\rho_{\rm c,0}/\chi$: the ambient column at $\chi=10$ is ten times that at $\chi=100$, and at $\chi=1000$ one tenth of it.

\subsection{Domain boundaries}\label{sec:boundary}
The grid calculations supply the wind at the upstream face and let gas leave through the other faces, while the Arepo, GIZMO and GADGET-4 calculations use periodic domains. The boundary control measures the effect of this difference within one method. Athena++ is run in three domains: with inflow and outflow, periodic along the wind with outflow at the transverse faces, and periodic on all faces. The solver, its options and the initial state are the same in all nine calculations, three at each sampling. In the fully periodic domain the total mass, momentum and energy remain constant to rounding precision. In the domain periodic along the wind, between 5 and 7 per cent of the total mass has left through the transverse faces by $5t_{\rm cc}$.

The lower panels of Figure~\ref{fig:boundary} follow the pressure of the gas approaching the cloud, averaged over the slab $-3R<x<-2R$ next to the upstream face. With inflow this gas keeps the wind state throughout. In the periodic domains it leaves the wind state at $1.1t_{\rm cc}$, the time a sound wave launched by the cloud needs to travel with the wind, at $v_{\rm w}+c_{\rm s,w}=1.5v_{\rm w}$, over the $16R$ to the downstream face, $16R/(1.5v_{\rm w})=1.07t_{\rm cc}$, and re-enter at the upstream face. From then on the cloud meets gas that has crossed the periodic face. Its pressure lies between 12 per cent below and 7 per cent above the wind value at $n=32$ and between 8 per cent below and 7 per cent above it at $n=128$, and its density falls up to 11 and 8 per cent below the wind value, respectively. In the fully periodic domain the bow shock also reaches the transverse faces, at about $0.6t_{\rm cc}$.

The dense fraction responds much later than the upstream gas. The three domains agree to within $4\times10^{-3}$ for the first $2t_{\rm cc}$ at every sampling. The periodic histories then decline more slowly than the inflow history: the difference first exceeds 0.05 between $3.2t_{\rm cc}$ and $4.1t_{\rm cc}$, depending on sampling, and reaches its largest value, 0.19 to 0.29, between $4.2t_{\rm cc}$ and $4.9t_{\rm cc}$. At $5t_{\rm cc}$ and $n=128$ the dense fraction is 0.104 with inflow and outflow, 0.272 in the domain periodic along the wind and 0.332 in the fully periodic domain; the corresponding values at $n=64$ are 0.022, 0.236 and 0.285 (Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}). The mass-weighted streamwise velocity of the dense gas is also lower in the periodic domains, by up to $0.06v_{\rm w}$ at $n=128$ and $0.11v_{\rm w}$ at $n=64$. In all nine calculations the dense gas remains upstream of $x=8.3R$, far from the downstream face at $17R$. No dense gas therefore crosses the periodic face, and the periodic domains act on the dense gas only through the gas that reaches the cloud.

Placed in the same domain, Athena++ and MFV end close together, while the other methods remain distinct. In the fully periodic domain Athena++ ends at 0.332 for $n=128$, close to the MFV value of 0.367, while GADGET-4 and MFM retain 0.764 and 0.976. In the domain periodic along the wind Athena++ ends at 0.272 and Arepo at 0.543. The periodic domains therefore raise the final Athena++ dense fraction by 0.17 and 0.23 at this sampling, which closes most of its gap to MFV and between a quarter and two fifths of its gaps to Arepo, GADGET-4 and MFM. The domain affects the earlier evolution much less: up to $3t_{\rm cc}$ the three Athena++ domains differ by at most 0.035 at every sampling (0.012 at $n=128$), and at $3t_{\rm cc}$ itself by at most 0.011.

\subsection{Density contrast}
The grid contrast histories are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:contrast}. At $\chi=10$, the final dense fractions are 0.722 for Enzo, 0.734 for Enzo-E and 0.621 for Athena++. AthenaPK gives 0.565 with the earlier HLLE/PLM configuration and 0.463 with the HLLC/PPM configuration. Both retain their initial tagged-material integral at the endpoint. Their modified corrections triggered four and 130 global first-order fallback stages, respectively. This numerical-configuration sensitivity accompanies changes in reconstruction, fluxes and correction source, whose individual effects require separate controls. At $\chi=1000$, Enzo and Enzo-E give 0.169 and 0.171, whereas the additional Athena++ calculation gives 0.0721. The latter uses $n=128$ with 16 mesh blocks and retains 86.2 per cent of its initial tagged-material integral and 92.5 per cent of its initial total domain mass at $5t_{\rm cc}$. Dense-gas membership therefore changes much more than the tagged-material integral in this calculation. These three measures are distinct: a dense-mass decrease alone does not separate mixing, dilution and transport through the boundaries. The larger spread in the high-contrast dense fractions motivates matched transport controls and a boundary flux budget.

The normalization by $t_{\rm cc}$ accounts for the initial contrast in the shock-crossing scale. For fixed radius and wind speed, the high-contrast calculation reaches a given $t/t_{\rm cc}$ after a longer absolute time. At $5t_{\rm cc}$, the $\chi=1000$ evolution has lasted ten times as long as the $\chi=10$ evolution. This distinction matters for the time available for transport through a finite domain.

Retained cloud-tag integrals, where available, provide a separate measure of material remaining in the domain. In the high-contrast Enzo calculation, 81.4 per cent of the initial tracer integral remains at the endpoint while only 16.9 per cent of the initial dense mass remains. Much of the tagged material can therefore occupy gas below the density threshold. A complete boundary flux budget would quantify the contribution of material leaving the domain.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{MFV evolution in relation to previous comparisons}
The GIZMO comparison shows a large separation at the finest sampling: MFM retains 0.976 of its initial dense mass at $5t_{\rm cc}$, whereas MFV retains 0.367. The MFV endpoints, 0.253, 0.409 and 0.367 as $n$ increases, also rule out a simple monotonic extrapolation from the two coarser calculations.

Resolution-sensitive MFV evolution has been reported before. \citet{Braspenning2023} found a slower late-time MFV decline at higher resolution for $\chi=10$; at $\chi=100$, their MFM cloud dispersed faster than their MFV cloud, and MFV closely followed Arepo. Our persistent MFM component has the opposite method ordering. Their meshless schemes were independent implementations in SWIFT \citep{Schaller2024}, with Mach 1.5 winds and a $40R\times10R\times10R$ periodic domain, whereas we use GIZMO, Mach 2 and a $20R\times10R\times10R$ domain. This comparison identifies a discrepancy that the tests below are designed to explain.

\subsection{Particle spacing and numerical support}
The initialization records identify a specific difference in spatial sampling. Our GIZMO cloud occupies a uniform lattice with variable masses, $m_i=\rho(r_i)\Delta x^3$. The cloud-to-wind mass ratio approaches 100 at the reference contrast. In the equal-mass construction of \citet{Braspenning2023}, the cloud has a greater element number density. At fixed wind spacing, equal-mass sampling at $\chi=100$ gives an idealized cloud spacing smaller by $\chi^{1/3}\simeq4.64$. The same nominal wind resolution therefore need not give comparable resolution of the cloud interior or its interface. This geometric difference is established, and the test below is designed to measure its contribution to the mass histories.

Particle spacing must also be distinguished from the numerical support used to reconstruct the fluid. In MFM and MFV the kernel defines a volume partition and a neighbourhood for reconstruction; it does not have the same role as the smoothing kernel in a conventional SPH density estimate \citep{Hopkins2015}. The relevant comparison should record the support-radius distribution, neighbour counts, particle masses and reconstructed density and pressure across the initial interface. Increasing the number of neighbours is not equivalent to increasing spatial resolution, and a smoother rendered image supplies no evidence that the hydrodynamic interface is better resolved.

At $n=128$, the adopted $0.1R$ transition spans only 1.28 initial lattice spacings. A pressure profile assigned from the analytic density can consequently differ from the pressure reconstructed by a particular solver. The GADGET-4 pressure departures show that this distinction matters in these calculations, and the test proposed below would measure it for GIZMO. A controlled test of MFM and MFV should compare equal-mass and variable-mass realizations, first matching the wind spacing and then matching the measured interface support. Pressure balance and the first update should be measured in each realization before varying the support prescription. Particle spacing and neighbour support are thus testable explanations for the present discrepancy.

\subsection{What the Arepo comparison adds}
Arepo supplies a useful comparison because it exchanges mass across explicit moving cell faces \citep{Springel2010}, while MFV uses effective faces defined by its meshless partition \citep{Hopkins2015}. Both can follow the bulk flow while permitting inter-element mass transport. This common feature motivates comparing their phase and mass evolution, but does not require agreement at finite resolution: the volume partition, gradients, motion and regularity of the elements can differ.

The measured Arepo endpoint is 0.325 at $n=32$ and 0.543 at both $n=64$ and 128. At $n=128$ it exceeds the MFV endpoint by 17.6 percentage points, while remaining well below GADGET-4 and MFM. The methods therefore span more than a simple division between fixed-mass and mass-exchanging schemes. In the same domain, periodic along the wind, Athena++ ends at 0.272, so Arepo retains twice the dense fraction of Athena++ under the same boundaries.

The finest Arepo calculation shows that its late evolution is still changing with resolution. From $0.1t_{\rm cc}$ the $n=128$ history lies above the $n=64$ history until the final time and declines 2.4 times faster over the last half cloud-crushing time. A natural next step is to measure the initial cell-volume and mass distributions and the reconstructed pressure across the cloud boundary, and to follow the same intermediate-temperature selections as for the meshless methods. Density and temperature structure, covering fraction and mass exchange should be assessed together; matching a final dense fraction could otherwise conceal different phase distributions.

\subsection{Boundary, transport and physical interpretation}
Periodic boundaries return disturbances and wake gas to the upstream side of the cloud, whereas inflow and outflow boundaries supply undisturbed wind throughout. The boundary control quantifies the consequence for Athena++ (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}): the periodic domains leave its dense fraction unchanged for $2t_{\rm cc}$ and raise it at $5t_{\rm cc}$ by 0.17 and 0.23 at $n=128$, with slower dense gas. Up to $2t_{\rm cc}$, when the twelve methods at $n=128$ retain between 0.82 and 0.95 of their dense mass, the Athena++ history agrees to within $4\times10^{-3}$ in all three domains. At later times the control accounts for part of the separation between the grid methods and the others; in a common periodic domain Athena++ and MFV end within 0.04 of each other, while GADGET-4, MFM and Arepo retain substantially more dense gas than Athena++.

The AthenaPK HLLC/PPM series extends the comparison with the earlier HLLE/PLM calculations; reconstruction, Riemann solver and correction source all change between those groups, so their differences measure the combined configuration.

The MFM and MFV diagnostics illustrate why several measurements are necessary: intermediate-temperature covering fractions remain similar despite a large dense-mass difference. Shocks, adiabatic evolution and numerical mixing can all move non-radiative gas through a temperature selection. Material tags combined with density and temperature would distinguish original cloud gas from ambient material that becomes dense. A measured local ambient velocity would also improve the shear estimate in the evolving cloud-crushing diagnostic. These additions would connect the recorded phase distributions more directly to entrainment and exchange.

For the radiative sample, resolving the cooling layer and distinguishing radiated energy, temperature-floor input and boundary transport remain necessary. The larger dense fractions with cooling are measured outcomes of the stated modified algorithms; matched controls with additional methods would test how generally they apply. The next experiments are the particle-construction and support tests, moving-mesh and particle-based calculations with inflow and outflow boundaries, and separate transport and cooling-resolution tests.

\section{Conclusions}
The dense fractions of the twelve methods lie within 0.12 of one another during the first two cloud-crushing times and spread widely afterwards. At $\chi=100$ and $n=128$, eleven of the twelve methods retain between 0.67 and 0.79 of their initial dense-gas mass after three cloud-crushing times. After five cloud-crushing times the eight grid methods retain between 0.10 and 0.28, MFV 0.37, Arepo 0.54, GADGET-4 0.76 and MFM 0.98. The late evolution of the grid methods continues to change between $n=64$ and 128, the MFV dense-mass sequence is non-monotonic with increasing resolution, and the two finer Arepo histories cross at the final time. The intermediate-temperature covering fractions of MFM and MFV, 0.201 and 0.184, are much closer than their dense fractions.

The additional grid histories extend the comparison to contrasts of 10 and 1000. Enzo and Enzo-E retain approximately 17 per cent of their initial dense mass at the high-contrast endpoint, compared with 7.2 per cent in Athena++. At this contrast the gas column at $5t_{\rm cc}$ extends across nearly the full width of the domain, whereas the $\chi=10$ clouds remain coherent; explaining the spread in dense fraction requires the matched controls described above.

Repeating the Athena++ calculation in the periodic domains of the moving-mesh and particle-based calculations leaves its dense-gas history unchanged for the first two cloud-crushing times and raises its final dense fraction at $n=128$ by 0.17 and 0.23. In a common periodic domain Athena++ and MFV end within 0.04 of each other, while GADGET-4, MFM and Arepo retain substantially more dense gas than Athena++ under the same boundaries. Explaining this remainder requires a controlled comparison of reconstructed pressure, particle sampling and numerical transport. The confirmed variable-mass initialization, the interface support and the GADGET-4 pressure departures identify concrete tests, and the different ordering of MFM and MFV relative to earlier work makes them particularly important. Establishing the effects of these choices is the next step towards using cloud-wind calculations to infer the survival and observable distribution of cold gas in galactic flows.

\subsection{Physical setup and cooling time}
A separate AthenaPK pair explores radiative cooling at $\chi=100$, $\mathcal M=2$, $R=100$ pc and $n=128$. The wind density and temperature are $10^{-27}$ g cm$^{-3}$ and $10^6$ K; the cloud values are $10^{-25}$ g cm$^{-3}$ and $10^4$ K. The wind speed is 305.76 km s$^{-1}$ and $t_{\rm cc}=3.20$ Myr. The mesh contains 128 blocks of $32^3$ cells. Both runs use inflow and outflow boundaries, HLLE fluxes, piecewise-linear reconstruction, van Leer integration and a $10^4$ K temperature floor.

The cooling table uses the solar-abundance equilibrium rates of \citet{Sutherland1993} in the implementation's $n_{\rm H}^2\Lambda(T)$ convention, integrated with the method of \citet{Townsend2009}. The equation of state assumes fully ionized H/He with helium mass fraction $Y=0.24$ and hydrogen fraction $X=0.76$. The mean molecular weight is therefore $\mu=(2X+3Y/4)^{-1}=0.588$. The fixed-composition equation of state and equilibrium cooling table are the adopted approximations.

An isobaric mixed-phase estimate gives $T_{\rm mix}=10^5$ K and $\rho_{\rm mix}=10^{-26}$ g cm$^{-3}$. Using the actual table value $\Lambda(10^5\,{\rm K})=10^{-20.87}$ erg cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, the thermal-energy cooling time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\rm cool,mix}=\frac{(3/2)n_{\rm tot}k_{\rm B}T_{\rm mix}}{n_{\rm H}^2\Lambda(T_{\rm mix})},
\label{eq:tcool}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\rm tot}=\rho/(\mu m_{\rm p})$ and $n_{\rm H}=X\rho/m_{\rm p}$. This gives approximately 0.24 Myr, or $0.075t_{\rm cc}$. The corresponding sound-crossing cooling length is about 12 pc, compared with a cell size of 7.81 pc. The cooling layer is thus sampled by only about 1.5 cells. This estimate characterizes the chosen mixed-phase state; the evolving flow contains a distribution of cooling times.

\subsection{Material tracer and flux correction}
The additional scalar is an advected cloud-material tag: its mass fraction distinguishes initially cloud-associated gas from ambient wind gas. It is transported with the fluid and measures material origin. The calculations remain in the imposed wind frame. Dense mass and tracer mass answer different questions because the former uses an evolving density threshold and the latter tracks the advected tag.

The custom correction acts on the numerical update when a candidate state violates density, pressure or tracer bounds. Shared interface fluxes are corrected consistently between neighbouring cells. In the last of four correction sweeps, persistent invalid cells trigger a whole-domain update using first-order local Lax-Friedrichs fluxes evaluated from the original stage state. The correction changes the hydrodynamic update during evolution. Both pair members use the same modified algorithm. The final fallback activated 23 times with cooling and 55 times without cooling, and can introduce additional numerical diffusion. The retained source change and activation counts are included in the supplementary code documentation.

\subsection{Pair results}
At $5t_{\rm cc}$, the cooling-on and cooling-off runs retain dense fractions of 0.882 and 0.105. The corresponding tracer fractions are 0.985 and 0.964. Figure~\ref{fig:cooling} shows their morphology and mass histories separately from the cross-code comparison. Figure~\ref{fig:cooldiag} gives the accompanying thermal and kinematic diagnostics.

The intermediate-temperature masses are 0.108 and 0.858 initial dense masses, while the covering fractions are 0.062 and 0.239. The selected dense-gas velocities are $0.278v_{\rm w}$ and $0.351v_{\rm w}$. Cooling changes the distribution among thermal phases substantially in this pair. Interpreting the magnitude requires resolving the cooling layer and measuring the energy supplied by the temperature floor, radiated away and transported through the boundaries. The two fallback activation histories also warrant comparison at higher resolution.
